Le Taillevent was the birthday dinner. Not a stop on the way to somewhere else. The kind of room you dress for, on rue Lamennais in the 8th, where the wood is dark, the linen is white, and someone is already walking toward you before you have finished saying your name.

We went on June 1, 2026. Giuliano Sperandio was still the chef. The house has since passed the kitchen to Philip Chronopoulos, so the plates will have changed. The feeling of the room has a longer memory than any one menu.

## Walking in

You feel taken care of before you sit down. They walk you to the table. They hand you the menu. Nobody hovers, and nobody disappears. If you need someone, they are already there, posted around the room the way people post bodyguards: quiet, dressed, paying attention.

They will even walk you to the bathroom. I loved that. It is a small thing, and it is the whole point. Classic French dining, the high end of it, is not a performance you watch. It is a sequence of gestures done so close to you that you stop looking for service at all.

The salon looks the way you hope a Paris institution will look. Greek-key woodwork, warm light in the ceiling, a long window covered in sheer white, a pink peony in a dark vase. Two Michelin stars, and the room still feels like a house.

## The menus

There is not one menu. There are a few tasting menus, and you choose at the table. In the first half of 2026 the house was offering its signatures side by side: Héritage du Taillevent, Gestes du Taillevent, a seasonal menu, and a longer menu built for sharing. Around then they were listed at roughly €245, €295, €270, and €345, before wine. Prices move with the season. Ask when you book.

2026 is also the restaurant’s 80th year. Through the end of July, Giuliano had a six-course anniversary menu at €345, a walk back through dishes the house is known for: watercress with oscietra caviar, a veal sweetbread in puff pastry with morels, crêpes Suzette flambéed in the room, and the chocolate marquise. One of those courses is built on Brittany crab. I could not eat it. More on that in a minute.

The card they set down is not only a list of dishes. It carries a line from the chef, and it tells you what the meal is trying to be.

I have a shellfish allergy. I said so, and the kitchen did not make a smaller version of everyone else’s dinner. They rebuilt the courses. The substitutes were not apologies. They were as good as the plates they replaced, which is the only standard that matters at a table like this.

## What came to the table

Dinner opened the way a great French meal still should: something warm, something small, a glass already poured.

Spring peas, twice: once in a silver bowl under a tangle of tendrils, once as a soft plate of peas and grains with the bread and the butter still on the table. Asparagus with flowers, the green so bright it looked poured.

## Cheese, then fire

The cheese course is not a plate someone composed in the kitchen and forgot about. It is a trolley, wheeled to you, with two people in dark suits who know every wedge on it. You point. They cut. The plate that lands is small and exact: a washed rind, a long slice of soft cheese, something with an ashed edge.

Gestes du Taillevent is not only a menu name. The dining room still cooks at the table. A copper pan, a blue flame, a sauce boat waiting. Crêpes Suzette is the one people come to see, flambéed in the room the way the house has done it for years. I liked the theater because it was not theater. It was a skill, done two feet from the linen.

## If you go

Le Taillevent is at 15 rue Lamennais, 75008 Paris, in the Golden Triangle, a former mansion that has been a dining room since 1946. It holds two Michelin stars. The cellar runs past 3,800 wines. Lunch is Tuesday to Friday. Dinner is Monday to Friday, from about 7:15. Book, and say the allergy when you book, not when you sit down. They can do more with notice, and they already do a lot without it.

I liked it. I would go back for the same reason I liked the first hour: you are treated like someone they were expecting. Not a celebrity, exactly. A guest. In a room this formal, that is the luxury.
